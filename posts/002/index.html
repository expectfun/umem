Post 002

A writer discovers dictation

Published 2026-11-15

Hi! This is the second blog post in my local model series. I've spent a week with my new laptop, which is capable of running some local models. While I have to admit that mostly I used cloud models because they are more capable, I'm beginning to find use cases where local ones are quite sufficient in their current form. Today I want to talk about one such use case. It's a kind of simple one, disregarded by many because it's not like a huge LLM giving you the answer to the ultimate question of the universe, but I think it's quite life-changing for people like me who have lots of incoherent thoughts and spend a lot of time usually typing them out and structuring them. The use case is speech-to-text.

So how do I go about it now that I have a decent laptop with 48 gigs of unified memory? The thing is, lots of projects use voice to some degree. Projects like ChatGPT proudly present their voice modes as full duplex dialogue. Some people complain that those duplex voice modes are dumber than their usual text counterparts, but currently I'm focusing on a simpler thing: speech-to-text. It's not a duplex mode; it's when you say things instead of typing them, and then they get transcribed into text. You send that text, and in all other regards, it's as if you had typed it from the start.

There's no dialogue with both sides interrupting each other; it's very asynchronous in its nature. But I find that it substantially changes my life, and it can be done by local models.

One example is that I am currently dictating this very post, but it's far from perfect. It's a trade-off because I don't have too much time for writing these posts by hand now, so I thought transcribed ones are better than nothing. But for user-facing text, typing is still better; it requires thought that would give people reading it a better experience. So if I had more time, I would definitely still write this blog by hand, but for the time being, I accept the trade-off and think it's better than posting nothing.

But then there's a second scenario, and it's a much better one: when what you write is not meant to be read by humans, but it's meant to be read by LLMs. So prompting LLMs, whether they are local or cloud, is still done by text. The thing is, first I care much less about all the imperfections there. I don't care that the LLM might be upset by my mistakes or incoherent ramblings. LLMs don't have feelings, and so for prompting them, less caring is needed.

I suppose they don't have feelings for now. I don't know what will happen in the years ahead; we'll see about that. Currently, I approach LLMs as if they don't have qualia, and I still try to behave decently around them just because it's natural for me. I don't want to tell them offensive things. I'm never angry with them; for me, it would be strange like being angry at a hammer. But I care less about being fully correct, and that's the thing I mostly do differently in regards to LLMs.

I care less about them having to work to understand me. When I write for people, I strive to be accessible and understandable. I don't want my readers to go through unnecessary work to understand what I meant. I want to write in such a way that a beginner in the topic would still be able to follow my thought without too much hard work, if possible. At the same time, an expert wouldn't be bothered by my oversimplifications, and the text would still mostly be correct. It's a fine line to tread; it's a lot of work when you write for an audience of more than one person. And even if I write for one person in a private message, I think of that person and how they would perceive something.

With LLMs, I think about it less. I think about it more with less capable models where you have to be very specific and clear so they would do anything valuable at all. But frontier models are quite good at capturing the context and understanding my half-baked thought. Even if it is rambling, even if the transcription from audio to text has some mistakes, they usually can understand what was really meant. So it turns out that you can dump a long, incoherent rambling into an LLM, and it will structure it and find what things require making decisions or answering questions.

For example, I might have some vision for a project, but it's not fully clear, and I just tell everything that's on my mind. The LLM mostly can go from that to some structured thing that outlines what we need to do and what shots I need to call.

So it turns out that those long rambling monologues can be very helpful. I would never type all of it by hand; it would be too much of a hassle. But sitting and talking, just saying everything that comes to my mind like I do right now, is very helpful. It really helps me understand what it is that I personally want, makes my head clear to some degree, and also helps me get on with the project and do things.

It's very helpful to have an option always available to translate your voice into text. Tools like Claude Code nowadays ship with a voice mode that you can use to do the things I'm talking about, and it is pretty great. I like it; it doesn't just transcribe blindly. It clearly takes into account the context and fixes things that could be transcribed wrongly at first, so I think Anthropic does a great job there. But it's limited to one tool, and when you start using that regularly, you start to feel that you want it always at your disposal regardless of what tools you're currently using. It feels like any form that you could type text into should come with the ability to do it by voice.

So there are projects exactly for that. Last year, they were mostly cloud-based. There are known ones like Wispr Flow and Superwhisper, and I also know a person who creates the Speak-Y app. I tried it, and I think that it's great; I recommend it. So if you need something like that with a cloud model, you can definitely use his version.

I get nothing from it financially; I just want to support a project that I like. At the same time, since I now can run local models, I want to try whether I can get away with using a local model. It's free, whereas cloud versions usually have a limited free tier, after which you have to pay. It's also more private, works offline, and so on. If it can be done locally well enough, then why do I need a cloud service that only puts more limitations on me?

The question was if it can really be done locally to a good enough degree. That's what I was trying to find out during the last week, and I've tried two projects. One better-known one is Handy. The other, less-known one is Hex by Anomaly, which I think creates the well-known OpenCode harness—don't quote me on that. I've installed both Handy and Hex. Currently, I like Hex a bit more, but I think it's a question of personal preferences.

They both, by default, use a Parakeet transcribing model, I think, but you can also download some other models. I've tried with the default one; I think it works well with English, but not all languages in the world. So I'm currently using it in English, but it can also download some models that are more multilingual. I want to say that in a week, I've grown quite fond of it. I really reach for it very often when I'm using frontier cloud models and prompting them, especially when I want to write a prompt that's longer than several words or involves paragraphs.

Then I do it via voice. It also turns out that when you're doing it via voice, you become more prone to creating long prompts. If I was still typing, I would settle for less; I would type as little as possible to save time. But when you're talking, you can go into more detail more easily, and you do. I think that's great because good prompting requires being detailed. People who prompt with very short prompts often get very similar, default, boring, bland slop. If you're trying to create a simple landing page and you just give the info to the agent and say "create a website," it will create a generic website similar to all the others. It's instantly noticeable; people dislike it—the same fonts, the same colors, and so on. But if you give a more detailed prompt, specify the fonts and colors, and are willing to iterate on that, then you get a much more personal, unique website. It's much more interesting, and it's much easier to do that with voice, I think.

It's both quicker, feels more natural, and lets your thoughts flow. It's kind of unusual for me; I've been writing my whole life and am very fond of putting words together in a way that's the best, most efficient, most understandable, and most accessible—the way that impresses people and makes them feel something. Writing words is a very powerful thing, but at the same time, I was maybe undervaluing voice and oral language. I value the time of people I talk to, and I feel that I can give them more by writing. But the time of LLMs is not so valuable, and my time becomes valuable. In that case, it might be much better to have incoherent rambling turned into a structured thing by an LLM than to sit and try to structure it yourself for a long time, if you just need something like a prototype for a landing page and need to iterate quickly.

Putting things together in writing is still very valuable for me, and I will obviously do it as long as I'm alive, I suppose. At the same time, it turns out that having a voice-to-text tool at your disposal all the time, along with frontier models to get the most of it—which is sadly not local yet, but I hope local models will get better in that regard over time—that's quite powerful.

Nowadays, I have a laptop where I can run Hex with its voice model without thinking about how much memory it uses. It just sits there quietly. If I have an all-consuming task, maybe I will quit it temporarily, but for the most part, I can just let it run in the background without thinking about it. I use a single shortcut: the Option key. I'll see if it clashes with some other hotkeys and becomes inconvenient in some cases, but for now, it feels okay. Just by hitting a single button close to me, I can go to the voice mode instantly, and that feels like a quality-of-life improvement.

I'm not sure which other local model capabilities I will use in the future, but I'm pretty sure I will use this one. If you haven't experienced it yet, I recommend you try it. Even if you've been writing your whole life, try talking. It's easy to laugh about all those vibe coders with their “rap coding,” where they talk to their laptops all day. It surely sounds fun, it may look funny, but still, I feel that it unlocks a lot of potential that can be otherwise wasted. That's all for now, I think, till next week when I'll talk about some other use cases. I'm also experimenting with image generation and so on, but we'll see about that next time.
